Beyond the Handmade Aesthetic: Why Craft Needs New Construction Economies
August 16
Audio available
Library of Muyinga / BC Architects. Image © Bc Architects
There has never been a more celebrated moment for craftsmanship in architecture. Across magazines, awards, and international exhibitions,
handmade brick
,
lime plaster
,
carved stone
,
woven bamboo,
and
rammed earth
have re-emerged as symbols of environmental responsibility, cultural continuity, and architectural authenticity. Beyond these carefully documented projects lies another reality. The overwhelming majority of buildings constructed today, from apartment blocks and office towers to public housing and commercial developments, continue to rely on industrialized systems that leave little room for skilled artisans. Craft has become increasingly visible at the very moment it has become increasingly uncommon. In countries such as
India
, the contrast is difficult to ignore.
Centuries-old building traditions continue alongside one of the world's fastest-growing construction industries.
To reframe the question of why craftsmanship is returning, perhaps the intrigue is why it now appears primarily where construction budgets, timelines, and clients can afford it.

How the Arts and Crafts Movement Still Shapes Contemporary Architecture
August 15
Audio available
Yardhouse / Assemble . Image Courtesy of Assemble
The
Arts and Crafts
movement is often remembered as
an architectural style defined by handcrafted details, an intimate scale, natural materials,
and a rejection of excess and industry. Its influence is frequently traced through
visual characteristics that continue to appear in contemporary architecture and interiors
, from exposed timber and
crafted
joinery to an emphasis on texture and permanence, yet reducing the movement to an aesthetic vocabulary overlooks its far more significant contribution. For
William Morris
, Philip Webb, John Ruskin, and their contemporaries, architecture was a question of
how things were made, who made them, and whether the process of making could cultivate dignity, meaning, culture, and beauty.

Applying Exposed Cork in Interior Architecture
Subscriber Access
|
August 14
Audio available
The Day After House / Takk. Image © Courtesy of Takk
How can we transport ourselves to natural environments when we find ourselves in entirely urban contexts? The materiality of spaces is one of the factors that determines the atmosphere we inhabit. In this regard, the incorporation of natural materials in interior architecture can help introduce textures, colors, and qualities inherent to nature into our daily spaces. In this article, we explore the specific use of cork as a resource in interior design and the different ways it can transform the experience of a space.
Cork
is the bark of the cork oak, a tree species that, when harvested without harming the tree, can be transformed into a material suitable for multiple applications in architecture and design.
